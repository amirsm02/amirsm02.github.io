The problem

Design letters that become a working molecule.

A protein’s sequence must fold into a shape that supports the desired chemistry. An enormous number of possible strings makes this a search problem.

What changed

A model helped design a fluorescent protein.

ESM3 proposed candidates; researchers selected and tested them. The resulting esmGFP fluoresced and had 58% sequence identity to the closest known fluorescent protein in their comparison. [2]

What it leaves open

One success is not a general success rate.

The experiment establishes a working design under the tested conditions. It does not tell us how reliably the approach will work for every new target.

The “500 million years” in the paper’s title is an estimate based on sequence divergence. It is not the model’s runtime or a replay of evolution.

01 Begin with the physical task

The letters have to do something.

A protein is a chain of The molecular building blocks linked together in a protein chain.. Biologists often write it as a string, using one letter for each amino acid. But the string is a description of a molecule: its parts interact, the chain takes a three-dimensional shape, and that shape helps determine what it does.

In green fluorescent proteins, a light-handling group called a The part of the molecule that absorbs light; in a fluorescent protein it can then emit light at a longer wavelength. forms within the protein. The surrounding barrel shields it. Incoming light can then be absorbed and re-emitted at a longer wavelength. Fluorescence needs illumination; the protein is not a tiny lamp producing energy from nothing. [1]

So “design a fluorescent protein” is a coupled problem. We need a sequence that folds into an arrangement that supports the right chemistry. Getting any one of those descriptions to look plausible is not enough.

Why can a small sequence change have a large effect?

A sequence position is not isolated from the rest of the molecule. A replacement can change packing, charge, flexibility, or a contact with another part of the chain. Some changes are tolerated; others disrupt the whole arrangement. This is why counting changed letters alone cannot tell us how well a protein will work.

A successful design must satisfy several constraints at once. How many possible strings are there to search?

02 Make the difficulty concrete

The search space multiplies.

Use the standard alphabet of 20 amino acids. A chain with two positions has 20 × 20 = 400 possible sequences. A third position multiplies that by 20 again. For a fixed length N, the count is 20N.

That counts possible strings, not working proteins. The design challenge is to put our search effort where useful molecules are likely to be. The fact that the full space is enormous does not, by itself, prove that any particular search method is good.

Why learning from existing proteins helps

Suppose we want a grammatical sentence. Sampling letters uniformly is wasteful because most combinations are nonsense. A language model can concentrate its proposals on combinations resembling patterns it learned.

Here is a tiny example of why relationships matter. Imagine two-position strings where only AA and BB are allowed. Choosing A or B independently with equal probability gives AA, AB, BA, and BB equally often: only half satisfy the rule. Instead, choose the first letter, then make the second match it. Both possible outcomes now satisfy the rule. This is an invented rule, not a model of a real protein; it isolates the value of conditioning one choice on another.

Protein design uses an analogous strategy, with a crucial extra test: a plausible sequence must become a working molecule. The training examples reflect physical constraints, but a model’s learned patterns are an imperfect guide to those constraints. A high-scoring proposal can still fail.

Does “new” mean the model ignored existing biology?

No. Learning from existing examples is the point. We should ask how far a design differs from the examples we know, whether its measured behavior is useful, and what the comparison actually establishes. A novel arrangement can arise from learned regularities without being a copy—and without demonstrating unlimited design ability.

Instead of trying every sequence, ask a model for completions consistent with a partially specified molecular design.

03 Understand the contribution

Prediction can become a design tool.

ESM3 learns from sequence, structure, and function represented as Think of a token as a symbol in the model’s vocabulary. It might encode an amino acid or a piece of structural information, rather than an English word.—discrete symbols encoding biological information. During training, some information is hidden; the model predicts the missing pieces from what remains. At generation time, a partial description can guide a new proposal. [2]

The direction matters. “Given a sequence, predict its shape” fills in one kind of blank. “Given part of the shape we want, propose a compatible sequence” fills in another. Neither instruction directly measures what a molecule does.

Why the laboratory step is part of the reasoning

We can formalize the distinction. Let s be a candidate sequence and let the model’s score be M(s). The property we actually want is a physical measurement F(s), such as fluorescence under specified conditions. Selecting a high M(s) is useful only to the extent that it predicts a useful F(s).

This gives experiments a second role beyond celebration. They reveal where the model’s proxy was wrong, and can identify a promising candidate for the next round. Selection and measurement are substantive scientific work.

Does a protein “language model” mean chatting in English?

No. Here the model operates on representations of biological information. A token may encode an amino acid or local structural information, rather than an English word. “Fill in the blanks” describes a training and generation principle; it does not mean every blank has one uniquely correct answer.

Now we can separate three claims: the model proposed it, the molecule worked in a test, and its sequence differed substantially from known examples.

04 Read the result carefully

Different letters. Measured fluorescence.

The researchers tested generated candidates, used a dim but distant design as the starting point for another round, and obtained esmGFP. They reported fluorescence intensity comparable to common GFP controls in their assays. This was a physical test, not just a structure prediction. [3]

The paper reports 58% The fraction of positions with matching amino acids in an alignment, using a defined comparison convention. to the closest known fluorescent protein in its comparison. That is a statement about matching amino acids in an alignment: sequences lined up for a position-by-position comparison. It is not a brightness percentage, and it does not mean that 58% of the molecule is new. [2]

Measured

It fluoresced.

The assay supports a functional protein under the conditions tested. It does not establish every property a biological tool might need, or superiority in every setting.

Compared

It was sequence-distant.

The reported 58% is relative to the closest fluorescent protein identified in the authors’ comparison. “Known” depends on the available sequences and the comparison method.

Estimated

“500 million years.”

The authors related this distance to divergence among natural fluorescent proteins. It is an evolutionary-timescale estimate—not the model’s runtime, a replay of evolution, or a dated ancestor of an artificial protein.

Why isn’t sequence identity the same as function?

Two strings can differ at many positions while preserving the interactions that matter for a particular function. Conversely, one strategically placed change can disrupt that function. The percentage deliberately discards where the differences occur and what they do. That makes it a useful summary of resemblance, but an incomplete explanation of mechanism.

What would a stronger general claim need?

A broad claim about dependable protein design would need evidence across many targets and conditions, with clearly defined success rates and appropriate comparisons. One compelling molecule can demonstrate a possibility without establishing how often the whole workflow succeeds. The right next question is about reliability and scope, not whether the successful molecule was “really” generated.

The idea to keep

A learned model can guide a search toward a working molecule far from familiar examples. The model supplies candidates; experiment establishes function; careful comparisons tell us what is new.
