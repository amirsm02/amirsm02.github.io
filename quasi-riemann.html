01 An irregular sequence, a regular trend

First, count the primes.

A prime has exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. The primes begin 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… They don’t arrive at a steady rate. But count all the primes up to a number x, and a surprisingly smooth pattern emerges.

Read the symbols: π(x) means “the number of primes up to x.” Here π names a counting function; it is not the number 3.14159… And ln means the natural logarithm.

The prime number theorem says that the ratio of the true count to x/ln x approaches 1 as x grows. This classical result is our starting point. The next question is about the remaining irregularity. [1]

Is the gap in this chart what the Riemann hypothesis bounds?

Not directly. The curve x/ln x is a deliberately simple first approximation. Precise Riemann-type error estimates use a better main term, such as the logarithmic integral Li(x), or a weighted count of primes. Don’t read the visible gap here as the error controlled by the new theorem.

02 A different way to look at primes

Meet the landscape of zeros.

The zeta function, written ζ, packages information about the primes into a function. Its inputs are complex numbers: points with a horizontal coordinate and a vertical coordinate. A zero is an input where the function’s output is exactly zero.

Write an input as s = β + it. For this picture, just read β as “across” and t as “up.” You can understand the question without knowing how to calculate ζ.

The zeros of interest lie in a vertical strip between β = 0 and β = 1. They are called nontrivial zeros; other, easy-to-locate zeros lie outside this view.

How does zeta know about primes?

In the region to the right of 1, the same function can be written in two ways:

ζ(s) = ∑n ≥ 1 1/ns = ∏p prime 1/(1 − p−s)

The right-hand expression runs over the primes. This connection comes from the fact that every integer greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization.

These formulas represent zeta when the horizontal coordinate is greater than 1. Inside the critical strip, mathematicians use a unique extension called analytic continuation. The point s = 1 is a pole, where the function is singular. [2]

What quasi-Riemann asks

Can we keep all nontrivial zeros a fixed distance away from the right edge, no matter how high we go?

What full Riemann asks

Do all nontrivial zeros sit exactly on the central line β = ½?

The released 7/8 result supplies a fixed zero-free region. It leaves much more room than the single line demanded by Riemann. 7/8 is a coordinate, not “87.5% of the problem solved.” [4]

What exactly does this picture leave out?

We show only the upper half of the critical strip, 0 < β < 1, where the nontrivial zeros lie. Conjugation reflects the zeros below the horizontal axis. A further symmetry also excludes β < 1/8 within the strip when β > 7/8 is excluded; we leave that consequence unshaded so the picture matches the stated right-hand boundary. The “trivial” zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are outside this view. “Inside the strip” is an introductory fact, not the strongest bound known before this release. [2]

03 Why a horizontal coordinate matters

Further left. Smaller irregularities.

Formulas connecting zeros to prime counts contain terms that behave, in part, like xβ. Compared with a main scale of x, the ratio is xβ − 1. A smaller β makes this factor shrink faster as we look at larger numbers.

In the model below, the purple curve falls fastest. It represents the central line, β = ½. The green curve falls more slowly, but still heads downward. This is why a fixed boundary away from 1 matters. [5]

Scale model, not an error guarantee. These percentages are the value of xβ − 1. They are not percentages of missing primes, measured counting errors, or the share of Riemann proved. Both axes use logarithmic scales. The β = 0.99 curve is a hypothetical comparison: the released bound rules out zeros that far right.

What has been simplified?

A zero ρ = β + iγ contributes a term involving −xρ/ρ to an explicit formula for a weighted prime count. For positive x, the magnitude of xρ is xβ. Our model shows only this power divided by x. The full expression also involves oscillation, the divisor ρ, many zeros, and additional terms. Real error estimates require controlling all of these together. [5]

04 Put the pieces together

The picture ends. The statement doesn’t.

Imagine a height far beyond the top of our chart, beyond any calculation anyone has ever done. The released statement still rules out zeros to the right of 7/8 there. That word everywhere is the leap from a collection of examples to a theorem.

No nontrivial zero lies to the right of 7/8, at any height. The restriction keeps going forever.

½

The remaining conjecture

Riemann asks for every nontrivial zero to be exactly on ½. Excluding a band near the edge doesn’t establish that.

The idea to take with you

Primes lead us to zeta. Zeta’s zeros constrain prime irregularities. Quasi-Riemann excludes a permanent region for those zeros. Riemann would pin them all to one line.

About the AI result and its verification scope

The OpenAI release’s scope note describes a Lean formalization of the 7/8 zero-free region for zeta and every Dirichlet L-function, as well as a specified family of Hecke L-functions. Poles are excluded. Later applications in the paper are outside that formalization. We illustrate zeta here; we have not independently rerun or audited the proof.

A shorter October 5 companion gives a different, weaker boundary of 11/12. That is not an improvement on 7/8: moving the boundary further left excludes more territory. Read the companion source ↗
